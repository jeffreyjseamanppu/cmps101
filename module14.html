Lecture: Emerging Technologies and the Future of Computing

1. What Makes a Technology “Emerging”?

An emerging technology is a developing or rapidly evolving tool or method that may change how people live or work. Emerging does not mean imaginary. Some technologies are commercially available but still limited, expensive, or changing quickly. Others exist mostly in research labs.

Ask four questions when evaluating any emerging technology: What problem does it solve? How does it work? What evidence shows it works? What trade-offs does it create? Separate a company demonstration, a peer-reviewed study, a pilot program, and a widely deployed product; they represent different levels of maturity.

2. Quantum Computing

Ordinary computers represent information using bits (0 or 1). Quantum computers use qubits, which can be prepared in quantum states involving superposition and entanglement. Quantum algorithms manipulate these states and produce measured results. This does not mean a quantum computer simply tries every answer at once, or that it is faster for every task.

Researchers investigate quantum methods for simulating molecules and materials, certain optimization and scientific problems, and specialized algorithms. Today's machines face noise, error rates, scaling difficulties, and significant engineering costs. Large, fault-tolerant quantum computing is still an active research goal.

Real-world example: IBM Quantum offers learning materials and access to quantum computing tools. Compare a classical laptop running a spreadsheet with a specialized quantum processor in a controlled laboratory. IBM Quantum Learning.

Security connection: Some sufficiently powerful future quantum computers could threaten widely used public-key cryptographic systems. Organizations are already planning transitions to post-quantum cryptography, which runs on conventional computers. Learn more from NIST's Post-Quantum Cryptography project.

4. Brain-Computer Interfaces (BCIs)

A brain-computer interface translates certain patterns of brain activity into commands for a computer or assistive device. Some BCIs use sensors on the scalp; others involve surgically implanted devices. Research teams are studying communication and movement assistance for people with paralysis or other severe disabilities.

A BCI does not read every thought, and present systems have substantial limits. Questions include surgery risks for implanted systems, accuracy, consent, access, long-term support, and the protection of sensitive neural data. Examples include work by academic research teams and companies such as Neuralink; distinguish clinical research from approved, widely available medical products.

Ethics prompt: If a device helps a person communicate, who should control the data it collects? Should employers or advertisers ever have access?

5. 3D Printing and Advanced Manufacturing

3D printing, also called additive manufacturing, creates an object from a digital design by adding material layer by layer. Unlike many traditional manufacturing methods that cut material away, additive methods build up the object. Common approaches use melted plastic filament, resin, or industrial materials such as metal powders.

  1. Create or obtain a digital 3D model.
  2. Prepare the model in slicing software.
  3. Print the object layer by layer.
  4. Inspect and finish the object.

Applications: rapid prototypes, custom fixtures, dental models, certain medical devices, and specialized aerospace components. Limitations: material strength, print time, cost, quality assurance, and safety. Not every 3D-printed object is suitable for medical or structural use.

6. AI Agents, Automation, and the Next Generation of Software

Traditional software follows programmed rules. Many AI systems recognize patterns or generate outputs from learned data. An AI agent may also plan steps and use permitted tools to complete tasks, such as searching information, organizing a workflow, or creating a draft. The level of autonomy varies by product and setup.

AI systems can make mistakes, expose sensitive data if poorly configured, or take unintended actions when given excessive permissions. Responsible use requires human oversight, clear boundaries, testing, and accountability. Connect this to Module 13: a physical robot senses and acts in the world; a software agent acts within digital tools and services.

7. Cybersecurity, Privacy, Energy, and Computing Careers

Emerging technology creates new security questions: Can an attacker manipulate sensor data? Could an AI system reveal private information? How are a headset's cameras secured? What happens when a device stops receiving software updates? Good security includes least-privilege access, software updates, encryption, backups, and careful handling of personal data.

Computing also has physical costs. Data centers require electricity and cooling; devices depend on materials, manufacturing, and eventual disposal. Compare benefits with energy consumption and e-waste rather than assuming every digital innovation is environmentally neutral.

Career connections: cybersecurity analyst, software developer, robotics engineer, data scientist, UX/accessibility designer, network engineer, quantum researcher, and digital manufacturing technician. Students do not need to become engineers to participate: policy, ethics, education, communication, and business skills also matter.

8. What Might Computing Look Like in 2035?

Possible developments include more capable AI-assisted workflows, better accessibility technologies, specialized quantum applications, improved robotics, and wider use of immersive training. These are possibilities, not guaranteed outcomes. A strong forecast identifies assumptions, barriers, and evidence. Ask what must happen for a technology to become affordable, reliable, safe, and widely accepted.

Critical thinking: Avoid claiming that every job will disappear, that quantum computers will replace laptops, or that brain interfaces can read arbitrary thoughts. Extraordinary claims require strong evidence.

Lecture Knowledge Check

1. What is the main difference between a bit and a qubit?

A classical bit has a value of 0 or 1. A qubit is a quantum system that can be prepared in a superposition of states and yields a classical outcome when measured.

2. How do AR and VR differ?

AR overlays digital content on the physical environment; VR immerses the user in a largely simulated environment.

3. Why do BCIs raise privacy questions?

They may collect sensitive information derived from neural signals, raising questions about consent, storage, ownership, and appropriate use.

4. What is one reason a technology demonstration may not lead to widespread adoption?

Cost, reliability, accessibility, regulation, user demand, or technical limitations may prevent scaling.

5. Why should predictions about 2035 include uncertainty?

Research progress, costs, laws, market demand, and safety outcomes are difficult to predict.

Hands-On Lab: Emerging Technology Reality Check

25 points · 60–90 minutes · No programming required

Goal: Evaluate the readiness of two emerging technologies using evidence, not hype. You may work in Google Docs, Word, Google Sheets, Excel, or a plain HTML document.

Step-by-Step Instructions

  1. Choose two different technologies from quantum computing, AR/VR/MR, BCIs, 3D printing, and AI agents.
  2. Find one reliable source for each. Prefer a university, government research institution, peer-reviewed article, or official technical documentation. Record the title, organization, URL, and publication/update date if available.
  3. Find one concrete application for each. Explain who uses it and what problem it solves. Identify whether it is a research demonstration, pilot, or established commercial use.
  4. Build the comparison table below. Write in your own words and distinguish observed evidence from predictions.
  5. Assign a readiness rating (1 = mostly experimental, 2 = limited pilots, 3 = available in niche settings, 4 = broadly used in some industries, 5 = widespread consumer use). Explain your rating. Different applications of the same technology may have different ratings.
  6. Write a 150–200 word conclusion identifying which technology you expect to affect everyday life more by 2035 and why.

Homework 1: Emerging Technology Research Report

50 points

Scenario: A community organization wants to understand an emerging technology before investing in it. You are the technology researcher asked to prepare a balanced introductory report.

Choose one: quantum computing, AR/VR/MR, BCIs, 3D printing, or AI agents. You may use the same technology as in your discussion, but your report must include deeper research and original analysis.

Required Report Sections

  1. Introduction: Define the technology and why it matters.
  2. How It Works: Explain the key components or process using simple language. Include a labeled diagram or original step-by-step workflow.
  3. Current Uses: Describe at least two real-world applications and identify the organizations or industries involved.
  4. Opportunities and Challenges: Explain at least three potential benefits and three limitations or risks (including at least one privacy, ethical, or security concern).
  5. Looking Ahead: Make one evidence-based prediction for 2035 and identify a major uncertainty.

Submit a 2–3 page report (excluding references) as PDF, DOCX, or HTML. Use headings and complete sentences. Include one diagram or workflow, which may be drawn using shapes, a slide, or a simple table. Cite sources in a consistent format. Do not submit AI-generated claims without checking them against sources.

Homework 2: Computing in 2035 – Your Future Technology Blueprint

75 points · End-of-semester synthesis

Scenario: You have been asked to brief a school, hospital, small business, or local community about what computing might look like in 2035. Create a realistic, engaging vision that connects concepts from across CMPS101.

Choose a Format

Submit either a 7–10 slide presentation (PDF or PPTX) or a 4–5 page illustrated report (PDF, DOCX, or HTML). Both formats must cover the same required content.

Required Content

  1. Setting and audience: Identify the organization or community and its technology needs.
  2. Five areas of change: Explain potential effects on education, healthcare, transportation, business/work, and daily life. Give at least one concrete example for each.
  3. Technology connections: Include at least four concepts studied this semester, such as AI, IoT, robotics, autonomous vehicles, cybersecurity, cloud computing, AR/VR, 3D printing, or BCIs.
  4. Now vs. 2035: Clearly separate capabilities that exist today from forecasts or speculative possibilities. Include a two-column comparison table.
  5. Benefits and risks: Explain at least three opportunities and three challenges, including cybersecurity/privacy, accessibility or fairness, and energy or environmental impact.
  6. Implementation roadmap: Propose three realistic steps the organization could take between now and 2035.
  7. Personal reflection: Write 200–250 words explaining which course topic most changed how you think about computing and one skill you want to develop next.
  8. Evidence: Include at least four credible sources with links, including at least one source that describes a limitation or challenge.
