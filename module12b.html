Full lecture: How smart devices connect our world

1. What is the Internet of Things?

The Internet of Things (IoT) refers to physical objects equipped with computing, sensing, and/or connectivity that exchange information with other systems. Some communicate over the internet; others communicate locally. A smart light bulb, fitness tracker, connected doorbell, or smart thermostat can be an IoT device. A basic remote-controlled appliance without networked data exchange may not be.

Everyday scenario: At 7 a.m., a scheduled routine turns on lights and adjusts a thermostat. A motion sensor can trigger the same light only when someone enters the room. The first is a time-based rule; the second reacts to sensor data.

3. Follow the data

Motion sensor → Local wireless network / hub → Automation rule → Smart bulb turns on

Not every action requires the cloud. Some setups work locally; others need a remote service or internet access. For a remote camera alert, the path may include a router, cloud service, and phone notification. Ask: Where is data processed? What fails if the internet goes down?

4. Amazon smart-home ecosystem

Echo / Alexa: Echo speakers and displays provide voice interaction and, depending on model, smart-home connectivity. Alexa can manage compatible lights, plugs, locks, thermostats, cameras, TVs, and robot vacuums. Echo Show adds a screen; Echo Hub focuses on smart-home control. Amazon Smart Plug switches power to a plugged-in device, but does not make that device's internal features smart. Fire TV / Fire TV Stick are entertainment platforms for apps and streaming, with supported voice and smart-home integrations. Ring video doorbells/cameras provide connected security features. Compatibility and subscriptions vary by product.

Example routine: A scheduled Alexa routine turns on a compatible lamp, gives a weather briefing, and plays music. Students should identify the trigger, actions, network dependencies, and data involved.

Reference: Amazon Alexa smart-home FAQs and Creating Alexa routines.

5. Google smart-home ecosystem

Google Home is the app and ecosystem for managing compatible devices. Nest speakers and displays support voice and home control; Nest thermostats manage climate; Nest cameras and doorbells capture and report events; Google TV Streamer and Chromecast with Google TV are examples of connected streaming hardware. Google TV is also software built into some televisions. A Google TV device is not the same thing as a Google Nest speaker, even though both may integrate with Google Home.

Example automation: At sunset, compatible smart lights turn on. Discuss whether the schedule is based on an online sunset calculation or a local clock, and whether remote control depends on cloud services.

References: Google Home device controls and Google connected devices.

7. How devices communicate

  • Wi-Fi: Common for cameras, TVs, speakers, and devices needing substantial network bandwidth.
  • Bluetooth: Short-range communication, often used in setup, wearables, and accessories.
  • Zigbee: Low-power mesh networking used by some sensors, lights, and hubs.
  • Thread: Low-power IP-based mesh networking for compatible smart-home devices; a border router connects a Thread network to other IP networks.
  • Matter: A smart-home interoperability standard that works over supported network transports, including Wi-Fi and Thread. Matter is not itself a replacement for Wi-Fi.
  • Cloud vs. local: Cloud services can enable remote access and advanced processing; local control may reduce latency and preserve some functions during outages.

Compatibility depends on device generation, software version, and supported standards.

8. IoT and artificial intelligence

Automation follows a defined trigger/action rule: “If the door opens after sunset, turn on the light.” AI may recognize speech, detect objects in camera footage, or predict patterns. A device can use both. Not all smart products use AI, and a voice assistant may rely on both cloud AI and simple rules.

Class exercise: Categorize these as sensor, rule-based automation, AI, or combination: (a) thermostat measures temperature; (b) plug switches off at 11 p.m.; (c) camera distinguishes a person from an animal; (d) voice assistant interprets “dim the kitchen lights.”

9. Security, privacy, and reliability

Risks include weak passwords, outdated firmware, exposed cameras, excessive data collection, insecure routers, vendor cloud outages, and products losing support. Protections include unique passwords, multifactor authentication where available, automatic updates, reviewing microphone/camera settings, limiting permissions, choosing supported products, and isolating untrusted devices on a guest or IoT network when appropriate. Check whether a product works locally if its vendor service stops.

Scenario: A smart doorbell sends video to a phone. What information is collected? Who can access it? How long is it stored? What happens if the internet is unavailable? Reference: NIST consumer IoT cybersecurity baseline.

10. Beyond the home: connected society

Smartwatches can track activity; hospitals may use connected monitoring equipment; factories may use vibration sensors for predictive maintenance; farms may use soil sensors for irrigation; retailers may use connected inventory sensors. IoT can improve accessibility through voice controls and alerts, but can also introduce subscription costs, accessibility barriers, privacy concerns, and dependence on vendors.

Reflection: Is a smart device always better than a traditional device? Support your answer with a specific example.

Knowledge check (ungraded)

1. Is a smart plug the same as a smart appliance?

No. A smart plug typically controls power; it does not provide access to the appliance’s internal functions.

2. Does Matter replace Wi-Fi?

No. Matter is an interoperability standard that can operate over supported networks, including Wi-Fi and Thread.

3. Does every IoT device need the internet for every action?

No. Some actions can run locally, while others depend on cloud services.

4. Is a scheduled light routine necessarily AI?

No. A fixed schedule is a rule-based automation.

5. Why do firmware updates matter?

They may address security vulnerabilities, reliability issues, and compatibility.
