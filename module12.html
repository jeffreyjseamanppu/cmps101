Internet of Things (IoT) & Smart Technologies

How everyday objects collect data, connect to networks, automate decisions, and create new opportunities and risks.

Format: 60-minute lecture · 40-minute lab · discussion · individual assignment

Module overview & learning objectives

From smart thermostats to factory sensors, the Internet of Things connects physical devices to digital systems. This lesson builds on earlier modules in networking, cloud computing, cybersecurity, data analytics, and artificial intelligence.

After this lesson, students should be able to:

  1. Define IoT and distinguish a connected device from a traditional device.
  2. Identify sensors, actuators, connectivity, cloud/edge processing, and user interfaces in an IoT system.
  3. Explain how IoT data travels from a physical device to an application or automated action.
  4. Compare consumer, healthcare, transportation, agricultural, and industrial IoT applications.
  5. Identify privacy, cybersecurity, reliability, and accessibility concerns.
  6. Design a basic smart technology solution with appropriate security controls.

Lecture 1: What is the Internet of Things?

Internet of Things (IoT) refers to physical objects equipped with sensors, software, and network connectivity that collect or exchange data. Some IoT devices also control the physical world through actuators, such as motors, locks, and valves.

Example: A conventional thermostat lets a person set a temperature. A smart thermostat can measure temperature, receive remote commands, learn a schedule, and adjust heating automatically. A device does not need AI to qualify as IoT.

Everyday examples

  • Smart home: connected doorbells, thermostats, lighting, and leak detectors.
  • Wearables: smartwatches measuring movement or heart rate.
  • Transportation: fleet trackers and vehicle diagnostic sensors.
  • Healthcare: connected monitoring devices that send readings to clinicians.
  • Agriculture: soil moisture sensors controlling irrigation.
  • Industry (IIoT): vibration sensors that warn technicians about possible machine failure.

Class prompt: Name three connected devices you have used or seen this week. What data does each collect?

Lecture 2: The five building blocks of IoT

1. SenseTemperature, motion, moisture, location, or light sensors measure conditions.
2. ConnectWi-Fi, Bluetooth, cellular, Ethernet, Zigbee, or other links carry data.
3. ProcessLocal processors, edge gateways, or cloud services interpret readings.
4. DecideRules or machine learning determine whether to alert or act.
5. Act / DisplayAn app shows results, sends notifications, or triggers an actuator.

Walkthrough: Smart leak detector

  1. A moisture sensor detects water under a sink.
  2. The device sends a reading to a home hub or network.
  3. A local rule or cloud service checks whether the reading indicates a leak.
  4. The homeowner receives a phone alert.
  5. If installed, a connected valve shuts off the water supply.

Important distinction: Sensors measure the environment; actuators change it. Some systems work locally even without an internet connection, while others depend on cloud access.

Cloud vs. edge: a useful trade-off

Cloud services can aggregate data across many devices, but they may require connectivity and incur ongoing costs. Edge processing can reduce latency and limit data sent off-site, but the device may have less computing power. Many systems combine both approaches.

Discussion: Should a smart door lock require a working internet connection to unlock? Explain your reasoning.

Lecture 5: Security, privacy & responsible design

IoT devices often operate for years, collect sensitive data, and may be difficult to update. A compromised camera, lock, or medical device can have consequences beyond ordinary data loss.

Common risks

  • Default or reused passwords and weak authentication.
  • Outdated firmware and devices that no longer receive security updates.
  • Unencrypted data or insecure cloud accounts.
  • Excessive collection or sharing of personal information.
  • Dependence on power, connectivity, and third-party services.

Practical safeguards

  1. Change default credentials and use unique, strong passwords.
  2. Enable multifactor authentication on supported accounts.
  3. Keep device firmware and mobile apps updated.
  4. Place less-trusted devices on a guest or separate IoT network when possible.
  5. Review data-sharing settings and disable unused features.
  6. Check vendor support policies before purchasing devices.
Ethics question: Should employers be allowed to monitor worker location or productivity through connected devices? What limits should exist?

In-class lab: Design a Smart System

Time: 40 minutes · Format: pairs or individual · No coding required

Scenario: A small business wants to reduce energy costs and prevent damage from water leaks. Design a smart monitoring system for the business.

  1. Choose a setting: retail shop, restaurant, winery, warehouse, or campus classroom.
  2. Choose at least three devices/sensors: for example, thermostat, occupancy sensor, leak sensor, smart valve, or energy meter.
  3. Diagram the data flow: sensor → connection → local/cloud processing → alert or action.
  4. Define two automation rules: “If X happens, the system will Y.”
  5. List three risks and explain one safeguard for each.
  6. Present a two-minute summary explaining costs/benefits at a conceptual level.

Lab worksheet

The worksheet is not automatically saved or submitted. Copy your answers into your course submission or print them.

Knowledge check

Answer the questions, then expand each answer to check your understanding.

1. Which component measures a physical condition?

A. Actuator   B. Sensor   C. Dashboard

Show answer

B. Sensor. A sensor measures conditions such as temperature or motion.

2. Does every IoT device need artificial intelligence?

A. Yes   B. No

Show answer

B. No. Simple rules can power many connected devices.

3. What is one advantage of edge computing?

A. Always unlimited processing   B. Lower latency for local decisions   C. No security risks

Show answer

B. Processing near the device can make responses faster.

4. Which is a good IoT security practice?

A. Reuse default passwords   B. Ignore firmware updates   C. Update supported devices

Show answer

C. Keeping supported devices updated reduces exposure to known flaws.

5. What is an actuator?

A. A component that changes the physical environment   B. A database   C. A type of internet connection

Show answer

A. Examples include valves, motors, and locks.

Discussion board: Is a Smarter World a Better World?

Connected devices promise convenience, safety, and efficiency, but they can also collect detailed information about our daily lives.

Initial post (200–250 words): Choose one IoT technology you would adopt and one you would avoid. Explain the benefits, what data the devices collect, and the security/privacy trade-offs. Should consumers have the right to use a smart device without sharing their data with the manufacturer?

Peer replies: Respond to two classmates (75+ words each). Offer a thoughtful counterpoint or an additional safeguard rather than simply agreeing.

Individual homework: Evaluate and Design an IoT Solution

Assignment: Research one real IoT product or system and propose a realistic use case for a home, campus, or small business. Your work must demonstrate how the technology operates—not simply describe its marketing features.

Required tasks

  1. Research a real device: Identify its manufacturer, product, intended purpose, and a credible product or technical source.
  2. Explain the technology: Identify the sensors, connectivity, data collected, processing location (device, hub, cloud, or combination), and resulting action.
  3. Create a diagram: Show data flow from sensor to decision to notification or actuator. A hand-drawn, photographed diagram is acceptable.
  4. Propose an implementation: Explain who benefits and give two automation examples using IF/THEN statements.
  5. Analyze trade-offs: Identify at least three security, privacy, reliability, or accessibility risks and propose safeguards.
  6. Evaluate the design: State one limitation, one potential improvement, and whether you would recommend adopting it.

Submission

Submit a 2–3 page report (excluding references and diagram), a labeled data-flow diagram, and at least two cited sources. Use your own words. If you use AI for brainstorming or editing, disclose the tool and how you used it, and verify any factual claims independently.
