CMPS101 • Introduction to Applied Computer Science

Robotics and Automation

How sensors, software, machines, and artificial intelligence work together to perform tasks in the physical and digital worlds.

Module format: Lecture • Hands-on lab • Discussion • Homework • Assessment

Module Overview and Learning Objectives

This lesson builds on previous topics in artificial intelligence, cloud computing, cybersecurity, and the Internet of Things (IoT). No prior robotics or programming experience is required.

After completing this module, students should be able to:

  1. Define robotics and automation and distinguish between them.
  2. Identify sensors, controllers, actuators, and feedback loops.
  3. Compare industrial, service, collaborative, and autonomous robots.
  4. Explain rule-based automation, robotic process automation (RPA), and AI-enabled automation.
  5. Describe benefits, limitations, safety issues, and ethical considerations.
  6. Design a basic automated system using a flowchart and decision rules.
Suggested schedule: Lecture 55–65 minutes; guided lab 35–45 minutes; knowledge check 10 minutes; discussion and assignment completed independently.

Lecture Part 1: What Are Robotics and Automation?

Robotics is the design, construction, programming, and operation of machines that can sense or act in the physical world. Automation is the use of technology to perform tasks with reduced human intervention. A robot may be automated, but not all automation uses robots.

Robotics example

A robotic arm lifts and positions parts on an assembly line.

Automation without a robot

Software automatically emails an invoice when a customer places an order.

AI-enabled automation

A vision system detects defective products and signals a mechanism to remove them.

Why it matters

Automation can increase consistency, productivity, accessibility, and worker safety. However, it can also introduce cybersecurity risks, equipment failures, high implementation costs, and workforce changes.

Lecture Part 2: How Robots Work

A typical robot or automated device follows a cycle: observe its environment, process information, decide what to do, act, and observe the result.

Sensor / Input→Controller / Logic→Actuator / Output→Feedback↺
ComponentPurposeExample
SensorMeasures the environmentCamera, temperature sensor, proximity sensor
ControllerProcesses inputs and applies instructionsMicrocontroller, industrial PLC, onboard computer
ActuatorProduces physical movement or actionMotor, gripper, valve
FeedbackReports results so the system can adjustWheel encoder, force sensor, limit switch
Power and communicationSupplies energy and connects componentsBattery, power supply, wired or wireless network

Example: Robot vacuum cleaner

  1. Sensors detect obstacles and floor boundaries.
  2. Software determines a safe path.
  3. Motors move the vacuum and operate brushes.
  4. Sensor feedback helps it change direction and continue cleaning.

Closed-loop control: The robot adjusts behavior using feedback. Open-loop control: The system performs an action without checking its outcome.

Lecture Part 3: Types of Robots

Industrial robots

Used for welding, packaging, sorting, and assembly in manufacturing.

Collaborative robots (cobots)

Designed for certain tasks near human workers, with appropriate safety controls.

Service robots

Help with delivery, cleaning, inventory, and other service tasks.

Medical robots

Assist with surgery, rehabilitation, and hospital logistics under human oversight.

Autonomous mobile robots

Navigate warehouses or other environments using sensors and software.

Drones

Perform aerial imaging, inspection, mapping, or surveying; autonomy varies.

Important distinction: A remotely operated robot is controlled by a person. An autonomous robot makes some decisions without continuous direct control. Most real systems have limits and require oversight.

Lecture Part 4: Automation in Software and Business

Automation is not limited to physical machines. Businesses use software to streamline repetitive work.

ApproachHow it worksExample
Rule-based automationUses predefined conditions and actionsIf inventory falls below 10 units, notify a manager
Robotic Process Automation (RPA)Software bots perform repetitive actions across applicationsCopy data from invoices into an accounting system
AI-enabled automationUses learned patterns to classify, predict, or interpret dataIdentify likely fraudulent transactions for review
Industrial automationControls physical equipment and processesAutomatically fill and cap bottles on a production line

Illustrative rule-based logic

IF temperature > 80°F THEN
    turn_on_fan()
ELSE
    turn_off_fan()
END IF

In real systems, engineers also add safety limits, sensor-failure handling, manual overrides, and alerts.

Lecture Part 5: Robotics + AI + IoT + Cloud

Modern automated systems often combine technologies covered earlier in CMPS101.

  • IoT: Sensors and connected devices collect and exchange data.
  • AI and machine learning: Models identify objects, recognize patterns, or help make decisions.
  • Edge computing: Processes time-sensitive information near the robot to reduce delay.
  • Cloud computing: Stores historical data, supports dashboards, and helps manage fleets of devices.
  • Cybersecurity: Protects devices, networks, software, and data from unauthorized access.
Example: A warehouse robot scans a package, checks its destination, chooses a route, moves the package, and reports task completion. Not every robot needs cloud connectivity; safety-critical control often happens locally.

Lecture Part 6: Benefits, Risks, and Responsible Design

Potential benefits

  • Improved speed and consistency
  • Reduced repetitive work
  • Safer handling of hazardous tasks
  • 24/7 monitoring where appropriate
  • Better accessibility

Potential challenges

  • Initial and maintenance costs
  • Sensor or software errors
  • Safety and liability concerns
  • Security and privacy risks
  • Job changes and retraining needs

Design principles

Use human oversight, emergency stops where appropriate, access controls, secure updates, logging, testing, and a safe response to equipment or sensor failures. Consider whether automation is appropriate before implementing it.

Guided Lab: Design an Automated System

Goal: Design a realistic system using inputs, rules, outputs, and safety features. No programming or physical hardware is required.

Choose one scenario

  1. Smart greenhouse: Monitor soil moisture and automatically water plants.
  2. Warehouse: Detect and sort packages by destination.
  3. Restaurant or winery: Monitor storage temperature and notify staff when conditions are unsafe.
  4. Campus: Control lighting in empty classrooms.

Lab instructions

  1. Describe the problem your system will solve (3–5 sentences).
  2. Identify at least two inputs/sensors, a controller, and at least one output/actuator or alert.
  3. Create a flowchart showing the input → decision → action → feedback cycle.
  4. Write at least three IF/THEN rules for your system.
  5. Explain what happens if a sensor fails or the network disconnects.
  6. Describe one security risk and one benefit to people or the business.

Example: Smart greenhouse

IF soil_moisture < 30% AND tank_has_water THEN
    start_irrigation_for_2_minutes()
ELSE IF tank_is_empty THEN
    stop_irrigation()
    alert_staff()
ELSE
    keep_irrigation_off()
END IF

IF sensor_data_is_missing THEN
    stop_automatic_irrigation()
    alert_staff()
END IF
Lab deliverable: A one-page design document with a labeled diagram, three rules, and short responses about benefits and risks.

Discussion Board: Will Robots Replace Human Jobs?

Automation can reduce repetitive work and improve safety, but it may also change the skills employers need.

Initial post (200–250 words): Identify one industry where robotics or automation is used. Explain two benefits, one possible impact on workers, and whether humans should remain involved in decisions. Support your view with a concrete example.

Peer responses: Respond to two classmates (at least 75 words each). Ask a thoughtful question or offer an alternative perspective.

Homework: Robotics and Automation in the Real World

Assignment: Research an existing robot or automated system and propose one improvement. Choose a system used in manufacturing, healthcare, agriculture, transportation, retail, hospitality, or another industry.

Required tasks

  1. Identify the system: Name the robot or automation solution, its manufacturer or developer, and where it is used.
  2. Explain how it works: Describe its inputs, decision-making process, and outputs. State whether it is rule-based, AI-enabled, remotely operated, or autonomous (some systems combine these).
  3. Analyze its impact: Explain at least two benefits and two limitations or risks.
  4. Propose an improvement: Recommend one realistic new feature or safeguard and explain why it matters.
  5. Draw a diagram: Show a simple flow of information or physical actions.
  6. Evaluate human involvement: Explain what should remain under human supervision.

Submission: A 2–3 page report (excluding references), one labeled diagram, and at least two credible sources. Include citations and links. Students should write in their own words and disclose any generative AI assistance according to course policy.
