Module Overview

Cybersecurity is the practice of protecting information, systems, networks, and services from unauthorized access, disruption, modification, or destruction. This module connects the networking and security concepts introduced earlier in the course to practical security decisions made by individuals and organizations.

Estimated workload: Lecture and videos (1.5–2 hours); lab (60–90 minutes); two assignments (2–4 hours total); discussions (45–60 minutes).

Ethical boundary: All activities in this module are defensive, simulated, and limited to systems or information you own or have explicit permission to assess. Do not scan, probe, or test another person's network or accounts.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this module, students should be able to:

  1. Define cybersecurity and explain why it matters to individuals, businesses, and critical services.
  2. Describe confidentiality, integrity, and availability (the CIA triad) using real-world examples.
  3. Distinguish threats, vulnerabilities, risks, and security controls.
  4. Recognize phishing, social engineering, malware, ransomware, and credential theft.
  5. Recommend practical safeguards including MFA, software updates, backups, least privilege, and security awareness.
  6. Outline the basic stages of responding to a suspected security incident.
  7. Communicate cybersecurity findings and recommendations to a nontechnical audience.

Lecture: Introduction to Cybersecurity

1. Why cybersecurity matters

Modern organizations depend on interconnected systems for communications, finance, healthcare, education, retail, and operations. A cyber incident can expose customer data, interrupt services, cause financial losses, and damage trust. Security is therefore both a technical and a business responsibility.

Class prompt: What would happen if your university's learning management system became unavailable for two days during finals?

2. The CIA triad

Confidentiality

Only authorized people can view sensitive information. Example: limiting access to student records.

Integrity

Information remains accurate and is changed only by authorized actions. Example: preventing unauthorized changes to grades.

Availability

Systems and data remain accessible when needed. Example: keeping registration systems running.

Teaching example: A stolen grade spreadsheet threatens confidentiality; secretly edited grades threaten integrity; a ransomware outage threatens availability. One incident can affect more than one principle.

3. Threats, vulnerabilities, risks, and controls

  • Asset: Something valuable to protect, such as a laptop, database, or customer record.
  • Threat: Something capable of causing harm, such as a scammer, malware, or accidental deletion.
  • Vulnerability: A weakness that could be exploited, such as an unpatched application or shared password.
  • Risk: The possibility and consequence of a threat exploiting a vulnerability.
  • Control: A safeguard that reduces the likelihood or impact of an incident.

Example: A business stores customer information on an employee laptop (asset). The laptop has no disk encryption (vulnerability). If it is stolen (threat), sensitive information could be exposed (risk). Full-disk encryption is a protective control.

4. Common cyber threats

  • Phishing: Deceptive messages designed to steal information or induce unsafe actions.
  • Social engineering: Manipulating people into disclosing information or bypassing procedures.
  • Malware: Malicious software, including spyware, trojans, and ransomware.
  • Ransomware: Malware that can encrypt files and disrupt operations, sometimes paired with data theft and extortion.
  • Credential attacks: Attempts to use stolen, reused, or guessed login credentials.
  • Denial of service: Attempts to overwhelm a service and prevent legitimate access.
  • Insider and accidental risks: Misuse of access, errors, or unintended disclosure.

Class prompt: Why might a convincing fraudulent email be more effective than a sophisticated technical exploit?

5. Defense in depth and everyday protections

Security is strongest when several safeguards work together. A business may use MFA, endpoint protection, software updates, backups, network segmentation, access controls, and employee training. If one layer fails, another can reduce harm.

  • Use unique, long passwords or passphrases and a password manager.
  • Enable multifactor authentication; phishing-resistant methods are preferable when available.
  • Keep operating systems, browsers, and applications updated.
  • Maintain tested, access-protected backups and a recovery plan.
  • Apply least privilege: give users only the access needed for their work.
  • Verify unexpected payment or password-reset requests through a trusted channel.

6. Incident response: a basic workflow

  1. Prepare: Establish contacts, backups, reporting procedures, and responsibilities.
  2. Identify and analyze: Determine what happened, what systems are affected, and how serious it is.
  3. Contain: Limit damage while preserving evidence and following organizational guidance.
  4. Eradicate and recover: Remove the cause, restore safe operations, and monitor for recurrence.
  5. Learn: Document lessons and improve security controls.

Scenario: An employee clicks a suspicious link and enters a password. What should the employee do immediately? Expected themes: report promptly, use a trusted channel, follow the security team's guidance, reset credentials safely, and review account activity.

7. Cybersecurity careers and responsibilities

Cybersecurity includes security analysts, incident responders, digital forensic specialists, cloud security engineers, security architects, governance/risk/compliance professionals, and security leaders. Technical knowledge matters, but communication, judgment, documentation, and teamwork are equally important.

Hands-on Lab: Cybersecurity Risk Assessment

60–90 minutes50 points

Purpose: Identify risks, prioritize safeguards, and prepare a short response plan for a fictional organization. No security software installation is required.

Scenario: Riverside Community Market

Riverside Community Market is a fictional small business with 12 employees. It sells products online and at a physical location. Employees share one administrator account for the online store. Some laptops have not been updated in months. Staff regularly receive unexpected invoice emails. Customer records are stored in a cloud folder accessible to all employees. The business backs up its files occasionally, but no one has tested restoring them. One employee reports clicking a link in an email claiming that the store's payment account will be suspended.

Lab Instructions

  1. Identify assets: List at least four assets the market needs to protect.
  2. Identify risks: Find at least five weaknesses or risky practices in the scenario. For each, explain the relevant threat and possible business impact.
  3. Apply the CIA triad: For three risks, explain whether confidentiality, integrity, or availability is threatened. Multiple categories may apply.
  4. Prioritize: Rate each risk as Low, Medium, or High and explain your reasoning in plain language.
  5. Recommend controls: Propose at least five realistic safeguards. Identify the three actions the business should take first.
  6. Respond to the suspicious link: Write a five-step response plan focused on reporting, protecting accounts, determining impact, recovery, and lessons learned.
  7. Summarize: Write a 150–250 word recommendation to the business owner.

Homework Assignments

Assignment 1: Cyberattack Case Study

50 points

Objective: Research a documented cyber incident and explain its causes, impacts, and lessons for organizations.

  1. Select a publicly documented incident involving ransomware, a data breach, phishing, or another security failure.
  2. Identify the organization, approximate incident date, and sources used.
  3. Explain what is publicly known about the attack and distinguish confirmed facts from speculation.
  4. Identify at least two affected CIA principles and explain why.
  5. Describe at least three security controls that might have prevented or reduced the incident's impact.
  6. Conclude with three lessons a small organization could apply.

Deliverable: 750–1,000 word report, including at least two credible sources and links or citations. Avoid reproducing exploit instructions.

Assignment 2: Personal Cybersecurity Improvement Plan

50 points

Objective: Apply cybersecurity best practices to a fictional personal digital environment or to your own environment without disclosing private details.

  1. Identify five common categories of digital assets (for example: email, cloud files, laptop, mobile phone, and social media).
  2. Describe one plausible security risk for each asset category.
  3. Recommend at least one practical safeguard for each risk, including a discussion of MFA, updates, password management, and backups where relevant.
  4. Create a prioritized 30-day action plan with at least six specific actions, approximate timing, and a way to verify completion.
  5. Write a 200-word reflection on the trade-offs between convenience, cost, privacy, and security.

Deliverable: 4-5 slide presentation. Use hypothetical examples and redact any personal details. Never submit passwords, recovery codes, account identifiers, or screenshots exposing sensitive information.

Discussion Questions

Discussion 1: Is Cybersecurity Everyone's Responsibility?

Many security incidents begin with everyday decisions such as clicking a link, reusing a password, or ignoring an update. Should cybersecurity be primarily the responsibility of IT professionals, or should every employee share responsibility? Explain your position using at least two specific examples. What training or safeguards should employers provide rather than placing all responsibility on employees?

Requirements: Initial response of 200–300 words; reply substantively to at least two classmates (75–100 words each).

Discussion 2: Security Versus Convenience

Imagine your university requires MFA for every login and automatically logs users out after periods of inactivity. Some students complain that these requirements interrupt their work. Should the university prioritize stronger security even when it reduces convenience? Recommend a balanced approach that considers accessibility, privacy, usability, and the risk of compromised accounts.

Requirements: Initial response of 200–300 words; reply substantively to at least two classmates (75–100 words each).
