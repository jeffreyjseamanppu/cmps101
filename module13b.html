Module Overview

Last module, we explored the Internet of Things (IoT) and how smart devices communicate. This week, we move from connected devices to machines that can sense, decide, and act. We will examine factory robots, home robots, medical robotics, warehouse automation, delivery robots, and self-driving vehicles—including Waymo, Uber, Tesla, Zoox, Cruise, and Pittsburgh-based Argo AI.

No programming or robot ownership is required. The emphasis is on understanding computing concepts, evaluating real technologies, and thinking critically about their benefits and risks.

Learning Objectives

  1. Explain the differences among robotics, automation, remote control, driver assistance, and autonomous systems.
  2. Identify sensors, actuators, processors, software, and feedback loops in a robotic system.
  3. Describe how AI, computer vision, mapping, and navigation support robotic decisions.
  4. Compare real-world robots in homes, hospitals, warehouses, and transportation.
  5. Explain how autonomous vehicles operate and why companies use different technical approaches.
  6. Analyze safety, cybersecurity, privacy, employment, accessibility, and ethical issues.
  7. Evaluate successful, discontinued, and changing autonomous vehicle programs.

Lecture Part 1: What Are Robotics and Automation?

Robotics combines computing, engineering, sensors, and physical machinery to create systems that can interact with their environment. Automation uses technology to carry out tasks with reduced human intervention; it does not always require a physical robot.

ConceptMeaningExample
Remote controlA person directly controls the machine from another location.RC car or remotely operated inspection robot.
AutomationPredefined rules or workflows execute tasks automatically.Factory conveyor or scheduled smart lights.
AutonomyA system senses its environment and makes some decisions independently.Robot vacuum navigating a room; a driverless robotaxi within its service area.
AI-enabled roboticsMachine learning or other AI methods help interpret data or plan actions.Robot identifying packages or pedestrians.
Key distinction: A self-driving car is not simply a remote-controlled car. Autonomous vehicles perform driving tasks using onboard sensing and computing; some fleets also have remote assistance staff, but remote assistance is not necessarily remote driving.

The Sense–Think–Act–Repeat Loop

  1. Sense: Cameras, lidar, radar, GPS, microphones, touch sensors, or wheel encoders gather information.
  2. Think: Software processes signals, estimates the environment, selects a response, and checks safety constraints.
  3. Act: Motors, steering systems, brakes, robotic arms, or speakers perform an action.
  4. Repeat: The system observes the result and adjusts through feedback.

Example: A robot vacuum detects furniture, updates its map, turns its wheels, and checks whether its route is clear.

Lecture Part 2: Major Types of Robots

Industrial and Warehouse Robots

Industrial robot arms weld, paint, package, and assemble products. Automated guided vehicles and autonomous mobile robots transport materials. Amazon fulfillment centers use robotic systems to move inventory and assist workers with repetitive workflows. Discuss the distinction between moving a shelf, picking a product, and making a decision about where to route an item.

Home and Consumer Robots

Robot vacuums and lawn mowers use sensors and software to navigate physical spaces. Smart speakers such as Amazon Echo or Google Nest are smart devices, but are not usually considered mobile robots because they do not physically move through the environment. Smart TVs, Fire TV, Roku, Apple TV, and Google TV are likewise computing and IoT devices rather than robots. This is a useful connection to Module 12.

Healthcare Robots

Surgical robotic systems can translate a clinician's movements into precise instrument motions; many are surgeon-controlled, not independent surgeons. Rehabilitation robots, pharmacy automation, and hospital delivery robots provide additional examples.

Humanoid and Legged Robots

Boston Dynamics' Spot is a four-legged robot used for inspection and research applications. Atlas and Tesla Optimus illustrate research and development in humanoid robots. Discuss why walking, grasping, balancing, and operating safely around people are difficult problems.

Drones, Agriculture, and Delivery Robots

Drones can inspect infrastructure or collect imagery; farm robots may monitor crops or assist with harvesting; sidewalk delivery robots navigate constrained environments. Different robots operate under different safety rules and levels of supervision.

Lecture Part 4: Autonomous Vehicles and Robotaxis

Autonomous driving connects nearly every major topic in introductory computing: sensors, networking, data processing, AI, cybersecurity, cloud systems, ethics, and human-computer interaction.

Driver Assistance Is Not the Same as Driverless Operation

SAE LevelGeneral DescriptionHuman Role
0–2Warnings or driving assistance; Level 2 may control steering and speed together.Human driver continuously supervises.
3Conditional automated driving in specified conditions.Human must respond when requested.
4High automation within a defined operational design domain (ODD).System can perform driving within that domain without a human driver.
5Full automation across all driver-manageable road conditions.No human driver required.
Safety note: Marketing terms such as “Autopilot” and “Full Self-Driving” do not by themselves establish a vehicle's SAE automation level. A Level 2 system still requires an attentive human driver. Capabilities, permitted operating areas, and commercial availability can change; use official sources for current status.

Waymo (Alphabet)

Waymo is a major example of commercial driverless ride-hailing. Its vehicles use multiple sensor types, detailed maps, and onboard software to operate within designated service areas. Students should investigate where it operates, what happens when a road is blocked, and how the company describes safety and remote assistance.

Uber: Building, Selling, and Partnering

Uber previously developed its own self-driving technology through its Advanced Technologies Group. Following a fatal 2018 testing crash, its program faced major scrutiny. Uber sold the self-driving unit to Aurora in a transaction completed in 2021. Its later strategy emphasizes partnerships and integrating autonomous rides into the Uber platform rather than developing all vehicle technology internally.

Tesla

Tesla develops automated-driving technology with a strong emphasis on cameras and neural networks. Discuss its consumer driver-assistance products separately from any specific robotaxi service. Students must distinguish a supervised system from a genuinely driverless service and verify the capabilities of the specific vehicle and location they research.

Zoox (Amazon)

Zoox has developed a purpose-built, bidirectional robotaxi design rather than simply adding autonomous hardware to a conventional car. Compare its passenger compartment and vehicle architecture with Waymo's modified production vehicles. Check current official materials for locations and public ride availability.

Cruise (General Motors)

Cruise operated driverless vehicle programs and encountered major safety and regulatory challenges. GM announced in December 2024 that it would stop funding Cruise robotaxi development and redirect its autonomous-driving strategy. Discuss how safety incidents, public trust, cost, and regulation can reshape technology investment.

Argo AI (Pittsburgh)

Argo AI, backed by Ford and Volkswagen, developed autonomous-driving technology and had strong Pittsburgh ties. The company shut down in 2022. This is a local case study of the gap between technical demonstrations and sustainable large-scale commercial operations.

Other Companies Worth Knowing

Aurora focuses on autonomous trucking technology; Motional has worked on driverless ride-hailing; Mobileye develops driver-assistance and autonomous-driving technology. These examples show that the field extends beyond passenger robotaxis.

Lecture Part 5: Safety, Cybersecurity, Privacy, and Society

Safety and Reliability

Robots must respond to unexpected events: a pedestrian stepping into the road, a sensor malfunction, construction barriers, a dropped package, or a person approaching a factory robot. Ask how the system detects a failure, enters a safe state, and informs humans.

Cybersecurity and Privacy

Connected robots can collect video, location, voice, and operational data. Risks include unauthorized access, insecure updates, compromised accounts, data retention, and attacks against networks or control systems. Useful defenses include authenticated updates, encryption, access controls, logging, segmentation, and careful data minimization.

Jobs, Accessibility, and Ethics

Automation may reduce repetitive or dangerous tasks while changing workforce needs. Robotaxis may improve mobility for some people, but access, disability accommodations, reliability, cost, and public safety remain important. Consider who is accountable when an automated system makes a harmful decision.

Think about it: Should the rules for a robot vacuum be as strict as the rules for a self-driving car? Why or why not?

Discussion 1 — Should Robots Replace People at Work?

25 points · Initial post: 200–300 words · Replies: two classmates, at least 75 words each

Robots are used in Amazon warehouses, factories, hospitals, restaurants, and delivery services. Some people argue they improve safety and efficiency; others worry about job loss, reliability, and the loss of human judgment.

Prompt: Choose one real-world robot or automated system. Explain what it does and whether you believe it should replace, assist, or remain supervised by human workers. Address at least two benefits, two concerns, and one example of a task that should still require human involvement. Include a link to a credible source or video.

Homework 1 — Compare Three Real-World Robots

50 points · Individual assignment · Submit a 2–3 page document or 6–8 slide presentation (not counting references).

Goal: Understand how different robots sense, decide, and act.

  1. Select three different robots from at least two categories (for example, Boston Dynamics Spot, an Amazon warehouse robot, a robot vacuum, a surgical robot, or a delivery robot). At least one must be a physical robot that moves through an environment.
  2. For each robot, identify its purpose, environment, sensors, actuators, and the tasks it automates.
  3. Explain whether it is primarily remote-controlled, automated, autonomous, or a combination. Justify your classification.
  4. Describe one benefit, one limitation, and one safety or privacy concern for each.
  5. Create a comparison table and a simple labeled Sense → Think → Act diagram for one selected robot.
  6. Conclude with a 150-word recommendation: which robot provides the greatest value and why?

Sources: At least three credible references, with working links. Use your own words.

Homework 2 — The Autonomous Vehicle Investigation

75 points · Individual assignment · Submit a 3–4 page report or 8–10 slide presentation (not counting references).

Goal: Compare the technology, business decisions, and safety challenges of autonomous vehicle companies.

  1. Research three companies: Waymo (required), one other current developer or service (Tesla, Zoox, Aurora, or another justified choice), and one historical or discontinued program (Uber ATG, Cruise robotaxi development, or Argo AI).
  2. For each, summarize the intended service, the technology approach (sensors, software, mapping where known), the human role, and the program's status as of the date of your research.
  3. Explain why an autonomous vehicle is different from an RC car or a Level 2 driver-assistance system. Include the meaning of an operational design domain (ODD).
  4. Compare at least three safety challenges: examples include construction zones, bad weather, pedestrians, emergency vehicles, sensor failures, or cybersecurity.
  5. Analyze the historical program: what went wrong or why did the company change strategy? Distinguish documented facts from your own interpretation.
  6. Draw a simple flowchart showing Sense → Perceive → Predict → Plan → Control → Recheck for a robotaxi responding to a pedestrian at a crosswalk.
  7. Conclude: Would you support a limited robotaxi pilot in Pittsburgh or your hometown? State three conditions you would require before launch.

Sources: At least four credible sources, including one government or independent safety source and one source documenting the historical company's outcome. Provide links and publication dates when available. Do not treat promotional claims as proven safety outcomes.
